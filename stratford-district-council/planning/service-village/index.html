Settlement hierarchy · National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF), August 2026

Service Village, Not Sustainable

A settlement hierarchy is a ranking of villages, made for the local plan. Under the 2024 NPPF it was a fair stand-in for "sustainable location". Under the August 2026 NPPF it cannot carry the point on its own. Whether a site is sustainable is decided on the route, under Policy TR3.

The tier still gets cited, but on its own it has never carried a site with a poor route. In every allowed case the inspector also had a bus service, lit footways or an urban setting to point to.

What it is

A ranking

The council scores its villages on size and a few services, then ranks them so the plan can send more housing to the better-served ones.

"It effectively ranks them so that housing is directed to the largest settlements with facilities…"Stratford Core Strategy Inspector, 2016, ¶271
NPPF, December 2024

A fair proxy

The Framework backed village growth and told decision-makers to allow for rural transport. A village's tier was a reasonable shorthand.

"opportunities to maximise sustainable transport solutions will vary between urban and rural areas, and this should be taken into account in both plan-making and decision-making."NPPF 2024, ¶110
NPPF, August 2026

Little weight on its own

That sentence is gone. Each site now gets a route test, and the tier cannot answer it by itself. The only rural wording left, TR3(1)(e), is a duty on the applicant to improve connectivity where it can, not an allowance for car use.

"limit the need to travel, particularly by private car, and offer a genuine choice of transport modes"NPPF 2026, TR3(1)(a)
"In rural areas, opportunities to improve walking, wheeling, cycling and public transport and enhance the connectivity of an area should be taken where they exist and can be supported by the development proposed."NPPF 2026, TR3(1)(e)

How much weight does the 2026 NPPF give the hierarchy?

There are four ways the tier could enter a decision. It survives as plan policy, and as a label inspectors cite beside route evidence. It decides none of them on its own.

In the Framework itselfwhole text
None
"Settlement hierarchy", "service village" and "service centre" never appear. The village-growth policy HO6(1)(b) is a plan-making policy, and ¶8 says "the plan-making policies should not be used when making decisions on development proposals."
As local plan policys.38(6), Annex A(2)
Plan accordance
A site inside a service village's boundary accords with the plan's spatial strategy, and that counts under s.38(6). The weight is the plan's, not the tier's: parts of a policy "which are materially inconsistent with national decision-making policies in this Framework should be given very limited weight." Stratford decisions under the 2026 Framework now treat Core Strategy policies CS.15, CS.16 and AS.10 in exactly that way.
Inside or outside a "settlement"Annex B → S4 or S5
Not the tier
Plan boundaries can count; the tier does not. And the definition "exclude[s] villages which lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt", whatever their tier.
Sustainable locationTR3, and GB7(1)(g)(iii) in the Green Belt
Not on its own
TR3 asks about a genuine choice of transport modes on the actual journey, and TR3(2) says the government's Connectivity Tool should be used. Inspectors do cite a village's tier when they find a location sustainable, but in every allowed case they also had a bus service, lit footways or an urban setting to point to. Where the route was poor, the tier did not save the site.
On the question that matters most, whether this site is sustainable, the hierarchy cannot carry the answer. The route has to.

What "Category 3" actually measured

Claverdon · Stratford's 2014 scoring

Shop1

Total 6 → Category 3 (5–7)of 12

Stratford's Local Service Villages (LSVs) are scored on four things: size, a shop, a primary school and public transport. Claverdon reached Category 3 on its school and its size.

On public transport it scored zero.

TR3 applies to every site, whatever the tier. But the lower the tier, the less the label says about getting around without a car. At Category 3 in Claverdon it says nothing, so the answer has to come from the route.

Expect the Tarleton argument at Claverdon; the answers are the route table below and a scheme that offers no footway, crossing or lighting.

And the tier is used against sites far more often than for them

43 decisions cite a low tier, an unlisted village or a site outside the hierarchy against the proposal — more than twice the 19 that cite a tier in its favour. Many route-decided dismissals carry no tier label at all even where the village is a named service centre; the inspectors went straight to TR3: 6006637 Hatton Station, 6007428 Halsall, 6008688 East Grinstead, 6009966 South Nutfield.

Before the 2026 Framework, three of four Council decisions on Claverdon reasoned “Category 3 Local Service Village with a shop, school, pub and surgery, so sustainable”; the fourth, 22/01896/FUL, called the same hinterland “an unsustainable location”.

Claverdon, 26/01470/FUL

Claverdon is a Category 3 Local Service Village in Stratford's Core Strategy and is washed over by the Green Belt, so it is not a settlement as the NPPF defines it (Annex B). The site is outside the village's built-up area boundary, so the tier gives it no plan support either. Five houses north of Station Road stand or fall on the route east to the services and the station:

On the routeWhat is there
FootwayNone on the site side of Station Road
LightingNone on the eastward route
Traffic40 mph limit from the village edge
CrossingNo formal crossing. The scheme offers a dropped kerb with tactile paving at the site frontage
BusFlexibus 510: one return journey, Wednesdays, on request
TrainUnstaffed request stop, no Sunday service

On the reasoning of Findon and Hatton Station, that is a car-reliant location, whatever Claverdon's place in the hierarchy. None of the allowed cases above had transport this thin: a Wednesday-only request bus and a request-stop station are not the "reasonable public transport" the Henfield inspector recorded, nor the lit footways, secured off-site works and bus contribution at Aston Clinton.

Expect the dropped kerb to be offered as the "planned improvement" that makes the location sustainable under TR3(1)(a), or as the connectivity enhancement TR3(1)(e) asks for. It is neither. It is access for the five dwellings: it serves no existing movement, so it enhances nothing for the area, and it leaves every deficiency on the route beyond it untouched. What inspectors have accepted under (e) is of a different order, such as the 20 mph order, traffic calming, bus shelters and crossing points secured at Chaldon. The kerb also tells against the scheme. It admits that every walk from the site begins by crossing an unlit 40 mph road, and the highway authority's audit review (8 September 2026, item 5.1) recommends "sufficient lighting be provided in this area to remove the dark spot around the pedestrian crossing point", while the ecology consultee asks that boundary hedgerows are "not illuminated (as far as possible)". The one improvement on offer needs a further improvement to be safe, and no document reconciles the two.
