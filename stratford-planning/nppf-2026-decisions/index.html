Stratford decisions under the 2026 NPPF: how consistent are they?

Stratford-on-Avon District Council decisions made between 17 August and 22 September 2026, compared with each other and with how Planning Inspectors have decided the same questions under the August 2026 Framework. Every quotation is checked against the source document. Prepared 28 September 2026. Not legal advice.

At a glance1. DP3(3)2. Forest Farm heritage3. CS.8 weight4. Green Belt location5. Walking routes and stations6. 2024 Framework reportsCase summariesGlossarySources

The National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) published on 17 August 2026 changed the tests that Stratford-on-Avon District Council (SDC) applies to planning applications. This note compares SDC decisions made between 17 August and 22 September 2026 in two ways: with each other, and with how Planning Inspectors have decided the same questions on appeal under the same Framework. Most SDC decisions reach results that follow from their facts. On one point SDC's approach is inconsistent with appeal decisions, and one SDC decision is hard to reconcile with its own findings. On the other points SDC's reasoning leaves out steps the Framework requires, or its method differs from the inspectors'.

Page numbers refer to the PDF of each officer report. Paragraph numbers (¶) refer to appeal decision letters. Every quotation has been checked against the source document. Each cited decision has its own summary page, linked from the text and listed under case summaries.

1. as a "should be refused" policy

In short. Alcester treated a failed "should be refused" policy as a reason to refuse (correctly). Snitterfield found significant harm to character and cited DP3(3), but used it only as a heavy weight in the balance (incorrectly), without asking whether the conflict was justified.

What happened. SDC has used the Framework's "should be refused" mechanism for flood risk but not for design.

  • Applied correctly: ✓ Alcester, 26/01376/FUL (refused 17 September 2026). Two houses in flood zones 2 and 3. The officer applied : "the proposal would fail against , and the proposal would fall into one of the specific reasons to refuse an application with the framework … Therefore the presumption in favour of development would not apply" (p.3).
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Snitterfield, 26/00617/PIP (committee 9 September, notice 10 September 2026). Up to five homes in a village washed over by the Green Belt, found not inappropriate under . The officer found: "I consider that the proposal would result in significant harm to the character of the area … In light of NDMP (3), I afford this harm substantial weight" (p.15). The conflict it identified was with local design policies: the scheme "would not accord with Policies CS.5, CS.9 and CS.12 of the Core Strategy" (p.15). As a permission in principle, the layout was not yet fixed. The report quotes in full, including its closing words "and applying paragraph 2 of this policy" (p.10). Its conclusion then weighs harm and benefits without applying : "I conclude that the benefits of approving the scheme would not be substantially outweighed by any adverse effects" (p.20). The report does not ask whether there was clear justification for the conflict.

What the Framework requires

DP3(3) Development proposals should be refused if, without clear justification, they conflict with paragraph 1 of this policy or relevant aspects of the principles in paragraph 2 …

S5(2) In applying this policy, the circumstances in which the benefits of approving development proposals are likely to be substantially outweighed by adverse effects include, but are not restricted to, situations where the development proposal would fail to comply with one of the national decision-making policies which state that development proposals should be refused in specific circumstances.

S5(5) … proposals should be approved unless the benefits of doing so would be substantially outweighed by any adverse effects, when assessed against the national decision-making policies in this Framework, and applying paragraph 2 of this policy.

  • DP3(3) is a "should be refused" policy in the same way as , which SDC treated as a trigger at Alcester.
  • DP3(3) applies to conflict with , with relevant DP3(2) principles, or with "explicit design standards set out in the development plan". Snitterfield found conflict with local design policies but did not say whether DP3(3) was engaged or ask whether there was clear justification. If there was not, S5(2) applies.
  • In the Green Belt, S5(5) applies S5(2) expressly.

Correct approach: ask whether there is clear justification for the conflict, and if not, apply S5(2) as Alcester applied S4(2)(c).

How inspectors have decided the same question

  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6011803, 126 Toms Lane, Kings Langley (Three Rivers), dismissed 9 September 2026. A Green Belt scheme found not inappropriate, decided under S5(5) (¶17). The scheme failed "one of the national decision-making policies, namely paragraph 3 of policy DP3, where there is a clear conflict with policy DM1 of the TRLDD insofar as it relates to the development of backland homes", and "This policy of the Framework directs that the proposal should be refused" (¶20).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6008785, Land adjacent Hanley House, Hook-a-Gate, Shrewsbury, dismissed 22 September 2026. was met (¶37), but conflict with the site's context engaged S5(2) (¶39), and "the benefits would be substantially outweighed by the adverse effects, when assessed against the national decision-making policies in the Framework" (¶42).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6008167, Land to the rear of Dovedale, Ware, dismissed 10 September 2026. "Paragraph 3 of Policy DP3 states that development proposals should be refused if, without clear justification, they conflict with paragraph 1" (¶21). A site inside a settlement, decided under ; the housing benefit was substantially outweighed.
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6005325, 61 Brackley Square, Woodford Green, dismissed 27 August 2026. Loss of a prominent tree was "contrary to the key principles in Policy DP3 of the Framework 2026, which include maintaining and enhancing tree cover" (¶23), and "Policy DP3 paragraph 3 states that development proposals should be refused" (¶30). A site inside a settlement, decided under S4.

2. Heritage harm at Forest Farm

In short. Ilmington weighed the heritage harm against the public benefits on its own terms and refused (correctly). Forest Farm found the same kind of harm but folded it into the overall balance (incorrectly), without the separate and s66 step.

What happened. Two one-dwelling permissions in principle, both affecting the setting of listed buildings and both decided while the Council could not show a five-year housing supply, were decided a month apart.

  • Applied correctly: ✓ Ilmington, 26/01399/PIP (refused 28 August 2026). The report records a supply of 2.21 years (p.5). It weighed the harm against the public benefits: "the provision of one dwelling is considered to be a modest benefit in the scheme of things" (p.10), and "On balance, I find the public benefits identified would not be enough to outweigh the harm caused to the character or appearance of the Conservation Area or the setting of the adjacent listed buildings" (p.11).
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Forest Farm, Long Marston, 26/01906/PIP (granted 22 September 2026). The report found "the proposals will be highly detrimental to the dwelling's setting" (p.7) and that the proposal "would be contrary to the statutory requirements of the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas " (p.8). In its conclusion the harm "is afforded substantial adverse weight, this is in conflict with of the Core Strategy and Policy HA.1 and ", and one dwelling towards "the currently substandard housing land supply" is "afforded substantial positive weight". It then concludes under that "the benefits of approving the scheme would not be substantially outweighed by any adverse effects" (p.10). The only reference to public benefits in the report is in its quotation of neighbourhood plan policy HA.1.

What the Framework requires

HE6(3) Any harm to a designated heritage asset will be a matter of considerable importance and weight, which should be dealt with in accordance with paragraphs 4 to 6 of this policy.

HE6(4) Where a development proposal would harm the significance of a designated heritage asset the effect on the asset and its significance should be weighed against any public benefits resulting from the proposal.

HE4(2) Any harm to, or loss of, the significance of a designated heritage asset (including from development within its setting) should have a clear and convincing justification …

  • HE6(4) requires the harm to be weighed against the public benefits, and requires "a clear and convincing justification" for it. That is a different exercise from the S4 test of whether the benefits are "substantially outweighed".
  • Forest Farm found conflict with HE6 and with the statutory duty, but reached its decision on the S4 test alone, without the HE6(4) weighing.
  • The weight given to one dwelling also differs: "a modest benefit" at Ilmington, "substantial positive weight" at Forest Farm.

Correct approach: Ilmington's. Weigh the harm against the public benefits openly under HE6(4), then carry the result into the S4 conclusion.

How inspectors have decided the same question

  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6007221, Mill Farmhouse, 7 Bridlington Street, Hunmanby (North Yorkshire), dismissed 4 September 2026. Weighs the harm on its own terms first: "clear and convincing justification has not been provided for the identified harm … the weight I attach to the public benefits which would result from the proposal, is not sufficient to outweigh the weight I attach to the harm to the assets and their significance" (¶40), and then concludes under S4 that "the benefits of approving the proposal would be substantially outweighed by the identified adverse effects" (¶51).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6008539, Land adjacent to Fulwood, Bagnall, dismissed 18 August 2026. A Green Belt site accepted as not inappropriate: "the proposal would not provide sufficient public benefits that would outweigh the harm" (¶13), and the Green Belt finding "does not in itself justify the grant of planning permission, nor does it override the identified harm" (¶15).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6006475, Water Lane, Smarden, dismissed 16 September 2026. Applies HE4(2) as its own requirement: "there is no clear and convincing justification for the harm to the significance of those assets" (¶59).

3. The reason given for discounting

In short. Inspectors at Wandsworth and Smarden kept local policies at their weight because there was no substantive basis to call them inconsistent (correctly). Four SDC reports gave CS.8 reduced weight (incorrectly), without saying which part conflicts with the Framework.

What happened. Three reports gave Core Strategy policy CS.8 (Historic Environment) reduced weight for the same stated reason: that adds criteria for assessing heritage effects that CS.8 does not contain. A fourth, Upper Quinton, said CS.8 "does not align" with the new policies.

  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Snitterfield, 26/00617/PIP. HE5 "provides a new set of criteria for assessments of the potential effects on development proposals on the significance of heritage assets which is not required within CS.8 of the CS and BE7 of the , I consider that CS.8 and BE7 are materially inconsistent with the of the Framework" (p.15). The same report then concludes that "the development would accord with NDMPs HE5 and of the Framework and Policy CS.8 of the Core Strategy" (p.16).
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Forest Farm, 26/01906/PIP. The same wording, ending "not required within CS.8, I consider that CS.8 is materially inconsistent with the NDMPs of the Framework" (p.6). The report's conclusion then relies on the conflict with CS.8 (p.10).
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Upper Quinton, 26/00922/FUL (refused 17 September 2026). "Core Strategy policy CS.8 does not align with the new NPPF HE policies and so I afford it limited weight in my assessment of this application" (p.4).
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Ilmington, 26/01399/PIP. The same wording, ending "I consider that CS.8 and HA.1 are materially inconsistent with the NDMPs of the Framework" (p.5). The report then refused partly for conflict with CS.8 (p.11).

What the Framework and the Core Strategy say

CS.8(B) Where proposals will affect a heritage asset, applicants will be required to undertake and provide an assessment of the significance of the asset using a proportionate level of detail relating to the likely impact the proposal will have on the asset's historic interest.

HE5(1) Development proposals affecting heritage assets should be accompanied by an assessment of the significance of the assets affected (including any contribution made by their setting) and of the potential effect of the proposal on their significance.

Annex A(2) Development plan policies (or parts of those policies) which are materially inconsistent with national decision-making policies in this Framework should be given very limited weight. … Other development plan policies … should not be given reduced weight simply because they were adopted prior to the publication of this Framework.

  • HE5 does add more detailed criteria. But already requires an assessment of significance and of the likely impact, and none of the reports explains why the extra criteria make CS.8 materially inconsistent rather than simply supplemented.
  • CS.8's test for harm short of substantial, that "this harm must be justified and weighed against the public benefits of the proposal, including securing its optimum viable use", is consistent in substance with and .
  • works part by part. The part of CS.8 that differs most from the Framework is its test for substantial harm. None of the reports identifies which part of CS.8 it considered inconsistent.
  • The weight given to CS.8 did not decide any of these outcomes, because HE6 and the apply directly.

Correct approach: give CS.8 weight except for any part that conflicts with the Framework, and state which part that is.

How inspectors have decided the same question

  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6006475, Water Lane, Smarden: "Policy HOU5 remains consistent with the broad thrust of the Framework" (¶66).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6007541, 7 Vicarage Mansions, Battersea (Wandsworth), dismissed 9 September 2026: "There is no substantive basis to regard them as materially inconsistent with national policy, and they therefore attract full weight" (¶4).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6008785, Hook-a-Gate: local design policies are "consistent with the Framework in requiring development proposals to respond to their context" (¶39).

4. Location shortcuts in the Green Belt

In short. At Kings Langley the inspector judged a washed-over site under the Green Belt tests, (correctly). Tanworth used the "existing settlement" test and Earlswood relied on the built-up area boundary alone (incorrectly).

What happened.

  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Tanworth-in-Arden, 26/00918/PIP (committee 9 September, notice 11 September 2026). Tanworth-in-Arden is washed over by the Green Belt. The adopted Core Strategy records that "The settlements of Alcester, Henley and Studley are excluded from the Green Belt" (¶4.1.3), and Tanworth-in-Arden is one of the villages the emerging South Warwickshire Local Plan proposes to exclude (Green Belt Exceptional Circumstances Topic Paper, version 2, July 2026, ¶176). The report found that "the development would be well-related to an existing settlement and would be of a scale that the local settlement could accommodate" (p.9), which is the S5(1)(j) test. The report went on to assess the scheme under , and this did not change the outcome.
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Earlswood, 26/01458/FUL (granted 3 September 2026). Only "the area where the dwelling itself is proposed" was inside the built-up area boundary (p.4). Limb (iii) of was passed as follows: "Considering the location of the dwelling, within the of an identified local service village, I am satisfied that the proposals would be in a sustainable location" (p.9). The report does not assess the route to services against .

What the Framework requires

Annex B, "Settlement" … For the purpose of this Framework they also exclude villages which lie within and are defined as part of the Green Belt in the development plan.

GB7(1)(g)(iii) The development would be in a sustainable location, with particular reference to policy TR3 of this Framework

TR3(2) The Connectivity Tool (Connectivity Tool - GOV.UK) should be used alongside other relevant quantitative or qualitative evidence in assessing the connectivity of particular locations proposed for development.

  • S5 "does not apply to development proposals in the Green Belt" (S5(5)), so the S5(1)(j) test is the wrong one. also means a washed-over village is not a "settlement", so cannot apply either.
  • A built-up area boundary is a policy line. asks about the location with reference to TR3, which calls for evidence about how residents would travel.
  • The Tanworth report did assess the walking route to the village under GB7; Earlswood did not assess its route.

Correct approach: apply and GB7 directly in the Green Belt, and assess limb (iii) on the facts of the route and the services.

How inspectors have decided the same question

  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6011803, 126 Toms Lane, Kings Langley. The council argued the site was within the built-up area, so S4 applied. The inspector noted "the site is designated in the Local Plan as being washed over by the Green Belt" (¶18) and applied S5(5) (¶17).

5. Walking routes and stations

In short. Inspectors decide location on the quality of the route for all users, and a station 350 m away did not save Hatton (correctly). Tanworth relied on residents coping and Earlswood assessed no route at all (incorrectly).

What happened. No two SDC decisions reach opposite results on comparable routes. That is because none of them tests location as expects, not because the routes were properly assessed.

  • Snitterfield, 26/00617/PIP. The committee report accepted "some harm that pedestrians will have to walk on the public highway" (p.9). The Update Report for the 9 September committee corrected this: "the centre of the village and services can be reached entirely on made footpath and do not require any pedestrians to walk in the public highway" (minutes and Update Report p.5).
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Tanworth-in-Arden, 26/00918/PIP. The report accepted that "some sections of that walk do not benefit from pavements", noting "the absence of pavements is for very short lengths only and on not especially busy roads and where speeds are low", and concluded "Villagers appear to cope with this existing situation without any great trouble" (p.9).
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Earlswood, 26/01458/FUL. Limb (iii) was passed on the built-up area boundary alone, with no assessment of the route (p.9; see section 4).
  • The Connectivity Tool. None of the SDC reports reviewed for this note uses it, although says it "should be used alongside other relevant quantitative or qualitative evidence".

What the Framework requires

TR3(1)(a) … This means the location should limit the need to travel, particularly by private car, and offer a genuine choice of transport modes for residents and users …

GB7(1)(g)(iii) The development would be in a sustainable location, with particular reference to policy TR3 of this Framework

GB7(1)(h)(i) Be within reasonable walking distance of a well-connected station (applying the definitions in the glossary at Annex B)

  • Inspectors decide limb (iii) on the quality of the route for all users: footway, lighting, width and traffic. The results go both ways on the facts.
  • The argument that residents already walk a route is the one the Hatton Station inspector rejected: use can reflect necessity rather than safety. Whether Tanworth's result would survive the inspectors' test is not assessed here. The point is that SDC used a different test.
  • A nearby station does not settle the question. Unless it is a "well-connected station" under , does not apply, and the station counts only through TR3, where the walk to it is judged like any other route.

Correct approach: assess the actual route to services and to any station, for all users, with the Connectivity Tool as TR3(2) requires. Existing use is not evidence that a route is safe.

How inspectors have decided the same question

  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6006637, Land off Oakdene Crescent, Hatton (Warwick), dismissed 23 September 2026. The site was about 350 m from a railway station, but "the station does not meet the Framework's definition of a 'well-connected station'" (¶18). "I also accept that the absence of continuous footways does not automatically render Station Road unsuitable for pedestrian use. Nonetheless … Station Road cannot reasonably be regarded as providing a safe route for all users, particularly pedestrians" (¶24). (This is Station Road in Hatton, not Claverdon.) On observed use: "it is likely that at least some journeys arise out of necessity rather than choice. In such circumstances, the recorded levels of activity do not demonstrate that the route is universally perceived as safe" (¶26).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6010313, Branford Wells, Brickhouse Lane, Newchapel (Tandridge), dismissed 4 September 2026. The lane "lacks footways and streetlighting", so "future occupiers of the proposed dwellings would be heavily reliant on private vehicles to access services, and the location would not offer them a genuine choice of transport modes" (¶14).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6011736, Land adjacent to Oaklands Drive, Copthorne (Tandridge), dismissed 22 September 2026. With a mainly unlit route, "journeys on foot and bike would not be attractive to the majority of occupants" (¶7).
  • Applied correctly: ✓ 6010471, Land south of Coppice Road, Poynton (Cheshire East), allowed 21 September 2026. The other way on different facts: "a continuous lit footway along a road subject to a 30mph speed limit … it nevertheless provides a safe and attractive opportunity for walking and cycling" (¶18).

6. Reports written under the 2024 Framework

In short. Inspectors applied the 2026 Framework to appeals already under way (correctly). Coverwell, Kineton and Oxhill kept the 2024 balance (incorrectly), and Coverwell decided the case wholly under the old Framework.

What happened.

  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Coverwell Farm, Pillerton Hersey, 25/01271/FUL (refused 17 August 2026). The report lists the "National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF) 2024 (revised 2025)" (p.2) and applies the 2024 paragraph 11(d) balance.
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Pittern Hill Riding School, Kineton, 26/01764/PIP (granted 25 August 2026). The report states "The lack of a 5 year housing land supply triggers NPPF paragraph 11(d)" (p.11) and runs the 2024 test. A closing note says "the balancing exercise undertaken in this report remains generally consistent and that when assessed against both the 2024 NPPF and 2026 NPPF, the decision remains one of GRANT" (p.13).
  • Applied incorrectly: ✗ Barn at Stonecroft House, Oxhill, 26/01393/FUL (granted 25 August 2026). The same closing note (p.10).

What the Framework requires

Annex A(1) The policies in this Framework are material considerations which must be taken into account in decision-making from the day of its publication.

  • The 2026 Framework replaced paragraph 11(d) with policies to . Outside settlements it allows only the categories, with for anything else.
  • Both reports list the August 2026 Framework, and the Oxhill report explains it "was written prior to the introduction of the 2026 NPPF". But a note that the old balance "remains generally consistent" does not show which 2026 test was applied.
  • The outcomes of these decisions may well be the same under the 2026 Framework; the point is the reasoning.

Correct approach: state the 2026 route (S4, an S5(1) category, S5(4) or and ) and apply its test.

How inspectors have decided the same question

  • Applied correctly: ✓ Every appeal decision cited in this note applies the 2026 Framework, including appeals made before it was published. At 6006637, Land off Oakdene Crescent, Hatton (Warwick), dismissed 23 September 2026, it "was published three days before the Hearing"; the inspector checked the parties had had time to consider it (¶2) and decided the appeal under it.

Glossary

Plain-English summaries, not quotations. Read the Framework or the Core Strategy for the full wording.

1990 Act
The Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990. Section 66 requires decision-makers to have special regard to preserving a listed building and its setting.
Annex A(1)
The 2026 policies are material considerations from the day the Framework was published.
Annex A(2)
Development plan policies, or the parts of them, that are materially inconsistent with the national decision-making policies get very limited weight. Others should not lose weight just because they are older.
Annex B
The Framework's glossary. Its definition of "settlement" excludes villages washed over by the Green Belt in the development plan.
BUAB
Built-up area boundary: a line drawn around a village in the local or neighbourhood plan.
CS.8
Stratford-on-Avon Core Strategy (2016) policy on the historic environment.
CS.8(B)
Section B of CS.8: requires an assessment of the significance of an affected heritage asset, and sets the tests for substantial and less than substantial harm.
DP3
The Framework's key principles for well-designed places.
DP3(1)
Development should respond to its context (the history, character and features of the site and its setting) and integrate with and enhance its surroundings.
DP3(3)
Proposals "should be refused" if, without clear justification, they conflict with DP3(1), relevant design principles in DP3(2), or explicit design standards in the development plan.
F7
The Framework's policy on making development safe from flooding.
F7(2)
Development in a location at risk of flooding should be refused unless five safety conditions are met.
GB6
Development in the Green Belt is inappropriate unless it falls within a GB7 category. Inappropriate development needs very special circumstances.
GB7
The categories of development that are not inappropriate in the Green Belt.
GB7(1)(g)
Development using grey belt land that meets four conditions: it does not fundamentally undermine the Green Belt, meets an evidenced unmet need, is in a sustainable location, and (for major housing) complies with GB8.
GB7(1)(g)(iii)
One of the four grey belt conditions: the development is in a sustainable location, with particular reference to TR3.
GB7(1)(h)
Homes or mixed use within reasonable walking distance of a "well-connected station" (defined in Annex B), meeting further conditions.
GB7(1)(h)(i)
The first station condition: within reasonable walking distance of a "well-connected station" as defined in Annex B.
HE4(2)
Any harm to the significance of a designated heritage asset, including from development in its setting, needs a clear and convincing justification.
HE5
How the effects of a proposal on heritage assets should be assessed.
HE5(1)
Proposals affecting heritage assets should come with an assessment of the significance of the assets, including their setting, and of the proposal's effect on it.
HE6
The Framework's policy on proposals affecting designated heritage assets, such as listed buildings.
HE6(3)
Any harm to a designated heritage asset is a matter of considerable importance and weight, to be dealt with under HE6(4) to HE6(6).
HE6(4)
Harm to a designated heritage asset should be weighed against the public benefits of the proposal.
NDMP
National decision-making policy: one of the Framework's policies for deciding planning applications, such as S4, DP3 or HE6.
NDMPs
National decision-making policies: the Framework's policies for deciding planning applications, such as S4, DP3 and HE6.
NDP
Neighbourhood development plan: a plan prepared by the local community, usually the parish council, which becomes part of the development plan once made.
S3
The presumption in favour of sustainable development. Apply S4 inside settlements and S5 outside them.
S4
Development inside settlements should be approved unless the benefits would be substantially outweighed by adverse effects, judged against the Framework's national decision-making policies.
S4(2)(c)
One of the situations S4 lists where the benefits are likely to be substantially outweighed: the proposal fails a national policy that says development "should be refused" in specific circumstances.
S5(1)
The list of development types that can be approved outside settlements.
S5(1)(j)
One category allowed outside settlements: development meeting an evidenced unmet need (for example housing where there is no five-year housing land supply) that is physically well-related to an existing settlement and of a scale that can be accommodated. A separate limb covers major freight development.
S5(2)
Outside settlements, and in the Green Belt through S5(5): failing a national policy that says development "should be refused" is a situation where the benefits are likely to be substantially outweighed.
S5(4)
Outside settlements: development not in any S5(1) category should be approved only in exceptional circumstances, where its benefits substantially outweigh the adverse effects.
S5(5)
The Green Belt uses GB6 to GB8 instead of S5. But where development is not inappropriate under GB7, it should be approved unless the benefits are substantially outweighed, applying S5(2).
S6
Housing that conflicts with a neighbourhood plan is likely to be substantially outweighed, if the plan is five years old or less and allocates sites to meet its housing requirement.
TR3
The Framework's policy on locating development in sustainable locations.
TR3(1)(a)
Development that could generate significant movement should be in a location that limits the need to travel by car and offers a genuine choice of transport modes.
TR3(2)
The Department for Transport's Connectivity Tool should be used, alongside other evidence, to assess how well connected a location is.
